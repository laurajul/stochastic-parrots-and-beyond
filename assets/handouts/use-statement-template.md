## 1. Intended use or justified non-use

- Which task do you want to do with generative AI, or which task do you
  decide not to do with it?
- Why do you consider generative AI for this task? What is the
  alternative?

## 2. System and data provenance

- Which system, version, and provider? How do you access it?
- What is known about its training data, its documentation, and its
  license? What is unknown?
- Which of your own data would the system receive?

## 3. Lifecycle risks and people affected

- Name at least two risks. Give a source or evidence from the course for
  each.
- At which lifecycle stage does each risk arise: design, data, training,
  deployment, evaluation, or governance?
- Who is affected: you, colleagues, research participants, data workers,
  the public?

## 4. Mitigations and less harmful alternatives

- What can you do to reduce each risk?
- Which less harmful alternative exists: another tool, a smaller model,
  a manual method, no use at all?
- Which mitigation can you carry out yourself, and which needs someone
  else?

## 5. Evaluation and limitations

- How do you check the outputs? On what sample, and by which criteria?
- What can the system not do for your task? What remains uncertain?

## 6. Disclosure, citation, and governance

- How do you disclose and cite the use in your publication or thesis?
- Which rules apply: your university, your faculty, your funder, your
  journal, the law?
- Which institutional owner must agree, or should know?

## 7. Defensible final decision

- **Constraints:** Which conditions must hold before you consider any
  use? Think of consent, data protection, and the rules that apply.
- **Comparison:** How do benefits, burdens, and uncertainty compare with
  the alternative, for the same task and the same quality?
- **Accountability:** Who decides, who can object, and when do you
  review the decision?
- What do you decide, and why? Which result would change the decision?

## Questions for your peer

These questions can guide the discussion. They are not graded. Ask your
peer whether the statement:

1.  **Adequately scoped use:** describes one concrete use or non-use,
    with the system and the task.
2.  **Relevant evidence:** names at least two risks, each with a source
    or evidence from the course that applies to this system and task.
3.  **Feasible mitigation:** proposes at least one mitigation or
    alternative that the author can carry out, and that addresses the
    cause of a named risk.
4.  **Transparent practice:** covers disclosure, citation,
    documentation, and the applicable rules.
5.  **Reasoned conclusion:** reaches a decision that follows from the
    analysis, names a check whose result would change it, and states its
    limits.

## Formal check (pass or fail)

The instructors are not experts in your field. They check the form only.
The statement passes when it meets all six criteria:

1.  **Length:** 1,200 to 1,500 words, without the bibliography and the
    peer note.
2.  **Structure:** the seven headings, each with your own text.
3.  **Sources:** a bibliography that lists every source that you cite.
4.  **Follow-up action:** one action, with what, by when, and who must
    agree.
5.  **Peer note:** the field and the role of at least one domain-expert
    peer, the date, the main feedback, and what you changed.
6.  **Data protection:** no confidential, personal, or unpublished
    research data.
